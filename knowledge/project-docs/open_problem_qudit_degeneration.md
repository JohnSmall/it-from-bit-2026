# Open Problem: Geometric Degeneration of Qudit State Spaces as a Consequence of the Division Algebra Constraint

## Statement

**Conjecture (Division algebra regularity theorem).** Every geometric regularity of the qubit state space that fails for qutrits and higher qudits can be traced to a specific property of the normed division algebras. Conversely, the full catalogue of geometric degenerations appearing at d ≥ 3 is generated by the violation of the division algebra axioms (alternativity, norm multiplicativity, absence of zero divisors).

More precisely: the state space Ω_d of a d-level quantum system exhibits a collection of geometric properties P₁, …, Pₖ (listed below) that hold if and only if d = 2^n for n ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3} and the state space is constructed from n entangled qubits via the Hopf fibrations associated with ℝ, ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆. For all other d, each property Pᵢ fails, and each failure is a consequence of the absence of a normed division algebra of the required dimension.

## The Properties

For a d-level quantum system, the pure state space is ℂP^(d−1), embedded in the space of density matrices via the Bloch representation ρ = (𝟙 + c_d **n**·**λ**)/d, where **λ** are the d² − 1 generators of SU(d) and c_d is a normalisation constant. The "generalised Bloch body" Ω_d ⊂ ℝ^(d²−1) is the convex set of all valid density matrices.

**P₁ (Spherical state space).** Ω_d is a round ball: all points at a given distance from the origin are valid states. 

- *Holds for d = 2*: Ω₂ is the Bloch ball B³ ⊂ ℝ³.
- *Fails for d ≥ 3*: Ω₃ is a proper convex subset of the ball in ℝ⁸, not SO(8)-invariant but only SU(3)-invariant (Goyal et al. 2016). Mixed states at a given Bloch vector length may or may not be physical depending on direction.

**P₂ (Pure states fill the boundary).** The set of pure states coincides with the full boundary of Ω_d.

- *Holds for d = 2*: Pure states are exactly S² = ∂B³, a single SU(2) orbit.
- *Fails for d ≥ 3*: Pure states form ℂP^(d−1), a 2(d−1)-dimensional manifold, which is a measure-zero subset of the (d²−2)-dimensional boundary ∂Ω_d. The boundary also contains mixed states of reduced rank.

**P₃ (Quadratic boundary).** The boundary ∂Ω_d is determined by a polynomial constraint of degree 2 in the Bloch vector components.

- *Holds for d = 2*: The constraint is |**n**|² = 1 (the sphere).
- *Fails for d ≥ 3*: The boundary of Ω₃ is determined by a *cubic* constraint arising from det(ρ) = 0. For general d, the constraint is degree d (the vanishing of the smallest eigenvalue of ρ), and cross-sections exhibit triangular, parabolic, and elliptic shapes that have no qubit analogue.

**P₄ (Sphere-to-sphere Hopf fibration).** The global phase quotient gives a fibration whose total space, fibre, and base are all spheres.

- *Holds for d = 2*: S¹ → S³ → S² (the complex Hopf fibration).
- *Fails for d ≥ 3*: The fibration S¹ → S^(2d−1) → ℂP^(d−1) has ℂP^(d−1) as base, which is a sphere only for d = 2. By Adams' theorem, sphere-to-sphere fibrations exist only in dimensions 1, 2, 4, 8 over ℝ.

**P₅ (Parallelisable total space).** The total space S^(2d−1) of the phase fibration admits a global frame (is parallelisable).

- *Holds for d = 2*: S³ is parallelisable (it is the unit quaternions, a Lie group).
- *Fails for d = 3*: S⁵ is not parallelisable. More generally, by Adams' theorem, only S⁰, S¹, S³, S⁷ are parallelisable — exactly the unit spheres in the four normed division algebras.

**P₆ (Transitive boundary action).** The adjoint action of SU(d) acts transitively on the full boundary ∂Ω_d.

- *Holds for d = 2*: SU(2) acts on S² transitively via the adjoint (= SO(3) on S²).
- *Fails for d ≥ 3*: The boundary contains states of different ranks, which lie on different SU(d) orbits. The orbit structure becomes stratified by rank, and SU(d) cannot map a rank-1 boundary state to a rank-2 boundary state.

**P₇ (Antipodal orthogonality).** Orthogonal pure states are antipodal on the boundary.

- *Holds for d = 2*: ⟨ψ|ψ⊥⟩ = 0 implies antipodality on S².
- *Fails for d ≥ 3*: d orthogonal pure states form a simplex (e.g. an equilateral triangle for d = 3) in a cross-section of Ω_d, not antipodal pairs. This reflects the fact that ℂP^(d−1) has a richer structure than S² — it contains non-contractible 2-spheres (for d ≥ 3) and has π₂(ℂP^n) = ℤ for all n.

**P₈ (Geometric = unitary equivalence of sections).** Two-dimensional cross-sections that are geometrically congruent are also unitarily equivalent.

- *Holds for d = 2*: All great circles on S² are unitarily equivalent (any two orthonormal bases are related by a unitary).
- *Fails for d ≥ 3*: Goyal et al. (2016) showed that there exist families of two-sections and three-sections of Ω₃ that are geometrically equivalent but *not* unitarily equivalent. This is a direct consequence of SU(3) being a proper subgroup of SO(8).

## The Conjecture in Detail

**Part A (Necessity).** For each property Pᵢ, exhibit the specific division algebra axiom whose violation causes Pᵢ to fail at d = 3. Candidate assignments:

| Property | Division algebra axiom violated | Mechanism |
|----------|-------------------------------|-----------|
| P₁ (spherical) | Norm multiplicativity | The SU(d) adjoint representation for d ≥ 3 does not act as the full rotation group on ℝ^(d²−1); the mismatch between dim SU(d) = d²−1 and dim SO(d²−1) grows with d. For d = 2 the "coincidence" dim SU(2) = dim SO(3) = 3 is underwritten by ℍ. |
| P₂ (pure = boundary) | Alternativity | ℂP^(d−1) has real dimension 2(d−1), while the boundary ∂Ω_d has dimension d²−2. These coincide only when 2(d−1) = d²−2, i.e. d = 2. For d ≥ 3, the boundary is "too big" for the pure states to fill. |
| P₃ (quadratic boundary) | Norm multiplicativity | The constraint det(ρ) ≥ 0 is degree d. For d = 2, det(ρ) = (1 − |**n**|²)/4, which is quadratic because the norm of a complex number is quadratic (|z|² = zz̄). For d = 3, the cubic term det(ρ) involves the symmetric d-tensor d_{jkl} of SU(3), which has no SU(2) analogue. |
| P₄ (sphere-to-sphere) | Existence of division algebra | Adams' theorem: S^n is an H-space only for n = 0, 1, 3, 7 (the unit spheres of ℝ, ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆). Sphere-to-sphere Hopf fibrations require division algebra multiplication to define the map. |
| P₅ (parallelisable) | Existence of division algebra | Adams' theorem again: parallelisability of S^n requires n+1 linearly independent vector fields, which exist iff n = 0, 1, 3, 7. |
| P₆ (transitive boundary) | — | This is P₂ restated group-theoretically. |
| P₇ (antipodal orthogonality) | Commutativity of ℂ | For d = 2, ℂP¹ ≅ S² has the property that the only pair of maximally distant points is the antipodal pair. For d ≥ 3, ℂP^(d−1) admits d mutually maximally distant points (a simplex), reflecting the non-trivial cohomology of ℂP^n. |
| P₈ (geom = unitary) | — | Follows from P₁: if the state space is a round ball acted on transitively by the structure group, geometric and group equivalence coincide. |

**Part B (Sufficiency).** Conversely, show that if a d-level quantum system's state space has the form Ω_d with all properties P₁–P₈, then d = 2^n for some n ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3}, and the system decomposes into n qubits whose entanglement structure is governed by the corresponding Hopf fibration.

**Part C (Graded degeneration).** For the qubit multi-party case (d = 2^n, n ≤ 3), establish which properties are inherited by the compound state space S^(2^(n+1)−1):

| n qubits | Total space | Hopf fibration | Properties preserved | Properties lost |
|----------|-----------|----------------|---------------------|----------------|
| 1 | S³ | S¹ → S³ → S² | All | None |
| 2 | S⁷ | S³ → S⁷ → S⁴ | P₄, P₅ | P₁ (S⁴ base is not the full state space; entanglement parametrises extra dimensions) |
| 3 | S¹⁵ | S⁷ → S¹⁵ → S⁸ | P₄ | P₅ partially (S¹⁵ is not parallelisable, but the fibre S⁷ is) |
| ≥ 4 | S^(2^(n+1)−1) | None | None of P₄, P₅ | All fibration-dependent properties |

This graded degeneration should mirror the loss of algebraic structure along the Cayley-Dickson chain: commutativity lost at ℍ, associativity lost at 𝕆, alternativity lost at 𝕊 (sedenions, which have zero divisors and hence cannot support a norm-preserving fibration).

## Known Results That Bear on the Conjecture

1. **Adams (1962)**: Only S⁰, S¹, S³, S⁷ are parallelisable. This establishes P₅.
2. **Adams (1960)**: Hopf invariant one maps exist only in dimensions 2, 4, 8 (over ℝ). This establishes P₄.
3. **Hurwitz (1898)**: Normed division algebras exist only in dimensions 1, 2, 4, 8. This is the algebraic root.
4. **Goyal, Simon, Singh & Simon (2016)**: Complete classification of two-sections and three-sections of Ω₃. Establishes the failures of P₁, P₃, P₈ concretely.
5. **Eltschka, Huber, Morelli & Siewert (2021)**: Three-dimensional model of the qutrit state space, demonstrating visually the departure from spherical symmetry (P₁) and the rank stratification of the boundary (P₂, P₆).
6. **Kimura (2003), Kimura & Kossakowski (2005)**: Bloch vector characterisation of N-level systems, establishing the general dimensional analysis for P₂.
7. **Bengtsson & Życzkowski (2006)**: Geometry of CP² as the pure qutrit state space, including the octant parametrisation and Fubini-Study metric.

## What Is New

The individual results above are known. What appears not to have been stated is:

(a) That the eight geometric properties P₁–P₈ form a *package* whose joint validity is equivalent to division algebra structure — not merely correlated with it.

(b) That the specific *pattern of failure* at d = 3 (cubic boundary, measure-zero pure states, triangular cross-sections, non-parallelisable total space, geometric ≠ unitary equivalence) can each be assigned to a specific violated division algebra axiom.

(c) That this has physical content: it is a mathematical theorem about *why qubits are the natural units of quantum information* — not merely a convention or a simplification, but a topological necessity if the state space is to inherit the full geometric regularity underwritten by the division algebra chain.

## Connection to the Framework

In the self-referential framework, the division algebra chain ℝ → ℂ → ℍ → 𝕆 arises from the Cayley-Dickson construction applied to self-reference. Each doubling introduces one binary (two-valued) distinction — one qubit. The chain terminates at 𝕆 by Hurwitz's theorem, giving exactly three internal qubits (A = ℂ, B = ℍ, C = 𝕆).

If the present conjecture is proved, it would establish that:

1. The qubit is the unique quantum information unit whose state space has full geometric regularity (all of P₁–P₈).
2. Higher-dimensional systems (qutrits, qudits) necessarily have geometrically irregular state spaces, and the specific irregularities are predictable from the division algebra framework.
3. The Born rule derivation from Hopf geometry (global phase = unobservable ⇒ U(1) invariance ⇒ minimum-degree quadratic ⇒ p = |ψ|²) generalises to all dimensions via the algebraic argument (which requires only the U(1) fibre, not the spherical base), but the *topological* derivation (which uses the specific topology of the Hopf fibration) is special to qubits — precisely because only qubits have the division algebra support.
4. The lattice gauge theory community's move to qudits for simulating gauge fields is an artefact of the lattice encoding, not a feature of the physics. The particle-native encoding (three binary Cayley-Dickson qubits per fermion) avoids qudits entirely, which the conjecture explains: the fundamental degrees of freedom live on division algebra spheres, not on ℂP^(d−1) for d ≥ 3.

## Suggested Approach

The most promising route may be through the *Tits-Freudenthal magic square*, which systematically relates the division algebras to the exceptional Lie groups and classifies all "nice" geometric structures over pairs of division algebras. The state spaces that appear as entries in the magic square (projective planes over composition algebras) have precisely the regularity properties P₁–P₈, while those outside it do not. Showing that the magic square *exhausts* the geometrically regular quantum state spaces would establish the conjecture.

An alternative approach uses the classification of *symmetric spaces*. ℂP¹ = SU(2)/U(1) is a symmetric space of rank 1. ℂP² = SU(3)/U(2) is also a symmetric space, but of a qualitatively different type: it has non-trivial cohomology (H²(ℂP², ℤ) = ℤ, H⁴(ℂP², ℤ) = ℤ) while ℂP¹ ≅ S² has only H²(S², ℤ) = ℤ. The richer cohomology of ℂP² prevents the boundary of Ω₃ from being a single orbit and forces the rank stratification. Formalising *why* the cohomological complexity of ℂP^(d−1) mirrors the loss of division algebra structure as d grows beyond powers of 2 would complete the proof.
