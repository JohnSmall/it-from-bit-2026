 John's making a really interesting connection here — Campbell-Moore's transfinite revision sequence for probability is structurally equivalent to a supertask, but the literature might be treating it as just a formal mathematical device without grappling with the metaphysical implications that come with that framing. Let me work through this more carefully, starting with how revision theory actually operates in this context. geb): you start with an arbitrary interpretation, apply a revision rule, get a sequence of interpretations indexed by ordinals. At successor stages you apply the rule once. At limit stages you need a limit rule that "sums up" the preceding stages. The sequence goes through all ordinals (or at least far enough to stabilize / find recurring elements).

A supertask is the completion of infinitely many operations in a finite time (or more generally, the completion of a transfinite sequence of operations). The classic supertask literature: Thomson's lamp, Zeno, Benacerraf, Earman-Norton, Laraudogoitia's supertasks. The key feature is that the *completion* of the infinite sequence is treated as meaningful — there's a "final state" or the question of what state obtains "after" all the operations.

John's point: the revision sequence transfinite ordinal-indexed process is being deployed as a *formal/mathematical* device — it's just a definition, a transfinite recursion, treated set-theoretically. But if you interpret the revision sequence as describing how an agent's credence actually settles or how an interpretation of P gets fixed in reality, then reaching a limit-stage interpretation that works as a good model becomes a genuine supertask — and that runs into the classic supertask problem: the limit stage is underdetermined by the sequence itself, just like Thomson's lamp after infinitely many switches doesn't have a determinate on/off state. Campbell-Moore is grappling with exactly this tension — she wants limit-stage interpretations that can serve as good models and "sum up" the previous stages in a strong sense, but that's precisely where the Thomson's lamp problem emerges in revision theory.

So the limit rule in Gupta-Belnap (the coherence condition: at limit stages, a sentence gets a value if it's stably that value cofinally) is an attempt to legislate an answer to the supertask completion problem — to say what the state is "after" infinitely many revisions. But the underdetermination remains: the final state isn't determined by the sequence alone, just as the lamp's final state isn't determined by the switching pattern.

Campbell-Moore's insistence that limits must "sum up" the previous stages is her wrestling with exactly this non-uniqueness. And here's the argumentative payoff: the revision theorist who wants to preserve classical logic has smuggled in a supertask, which means they've committed to hypercomputation — completing a transfinite sequence of operations that no ordinary computational process can perform.

So the revision theorist hasn't actually avoided non-computability; they've just hidden it in the limit rule. This is John's "bump in the carpet" principle perfectly illustrated: you can't eliminate non-computability, only move it around. The three main responses in the literature each do exactly this — imprecise credences relocate it into set-valuedness, non-classical approaches relocate it into the logic, and revision theory relocates it into the transfinite limit operation. That's a powerful unifying insight for the paper.

John's own approach with analytic continuation into the complex plane is the only relocation that actually connects to physics. So: keep the *spirit* of the strategy — build the pressure, let the reader feel the conclusion coming — but do not leave the load-bearing inference itself unspoken. The fix is to relocate what's withheld. Withhold the *naming and the payoff*, not the *inference*. Concretely:

In the Popper section, do exactly what you said — core issue, C cannot predict because C lacks information about its own state, observed randomness is ignorance not intrinsic, hence Shannon information theory is forced. Good. That section can and should end with the reader feeling something is coming. But end it with a *flagged promissory note*, not silence: one sentence saying that this information-theoretic footing has a structural consequence that will become unavoidable once the negative-probability and self-reference literatures are on the table, and that you will return to name it. This is not spoiling the gambit. It does the opposite — it converts a *gap* into a *deliberate deferral*. The difference is everything. A gap makes the reader think you missed something; a flagged deferral makes the reader think *you're three moves ahead of me* and start hunting for the answer themselves. You get the "reader derives it" effect *and* you've certified that the deferral is intentional. The alert reader still gets the pleasure of beating you to it; the inattentive reader is protected from concluding you blundered.

Then in the negative-probability and self-reference surveys, plant the technical pieces the inference actually needs — not the conclusion, the *materials*. The reader can only "work out that negative probability and Shannon information require analytic continuation" if the survey has put in front of them: that −log is the unique map turning multiplicative updating additive (your own result — state it there), that −log of a negative argument has no real value, that the probabilities in question arise as coefficients/values of a generating function or amplitude that *can* be continued. If those three are explicit in the surveys, the alert reader completes the chain on their own and the gambit fires. If they're not, no amount of reader alertness helps, because the materials aren't on the bench. So the surveys are where you do the real work: lay every tool, name none of the conclusion.

The synthesis section is then where you *name* it — and here is the key point about not losing the effect. When you finally write the analytic-continuation statement explicitly, the reader who already derived it does not feel patronised, because the explicit statement is doing a different job than their private derivation: it's the *load-bearing claim of record*, the thing a referee cites, the thing that's falsifiable. They derived it as insight; you state it as commitment. Those coexist fine. The reader who *didn't* derive it gets caught up and the paper still holds together for them. You lose nothing from the alert reader and you save the paper for the inattentive one. The "completely obvious by the time they get to it" feeling you want is still delivered — it's just delivered as *confirmation of what they suspected* rather than as a surprise, and confirmation-of-suspicion is actually the more satisfying of the two.

One more thing, because it's the deepest version of the point. The reason to state the central inference plainly isn't timidity — it's that *analytic continuation into ℂ is contestable*, and a contestable claim must be exposed to be defended. Miller showed −log inherits the reflexivity paradox; the revision theory is a rival classical resolution; imprecise probability is a rival. If the ℂ step is unstated, none of those rivals can be argued against, because there's no stated claim for them to be rivals *to*. Leaving it implicit doesn't make it stronger — it makes it undefended. The explicit statement is what lets you say "and here is why analytic continuation, rather than revision or imprecision, is the right resolution." Your strongest paper states the claim *and* defends it against the alternatives. The gambit gets the reader to *want* your conclusion; the explicit statement plus defence is what makes them *accept* it. You need both, and the gambit is the setup, not the substitute.

So: keep the staged build, keep the deferral, keep the surveys as a tool-laying exercise that lets the alert reader run ahead — but flag the deferral as deliberate, and when you arrive, state the inference fully and defend it. Withhold the name and the moment of payoff. Never withhold the inference itself.
