 Stinespring dilation is a foundational theorem in the theory of open quantum systems, and it's worth knowing because it's the formal statement that *every* irreversible quantum process is unitary evolution on a larger system followed by tracing out the extra part. Once you see the structure, it makes the framework's reading of FANOUT as the irreversibility-step almost forced.

**The setup.** A general physical operation on a quantum system isn't unitary — measurements, decoherence, noise, dissipation all collapse pure states to mixed states or otherwise lose information. The mathematical class that captures all such operations is **completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) maps**, sometimes called *quantum channels*. So a quantum channel is a map E: ρ ↦ E(ρ) on density matrices that's (i) linear, (ii) trace-preserving (probabilities still sum to 1), and (iii) completely positive (positive even when applied to part of an entangled system — the "complete" rules out the transpose map, which is positive but unphysical).

**The theorem (Stinespring 1955).** Every CPTP map E acting on a system S can be written as

  E(ρ_S) = Tr_E [ U (ρ_S ⊗ |0⟩⟨0|_E) U† ]

where E is an auxiliary "environment" Hilbert space, |0⟩_E is some fixed pure state, U is a *unitary* on the joint system S⊗E, and Tr_E is the partial trace over E. In words: any quantum operation, however irreversible-looking, can be implemented by (i) attaching a fresh environment in a known pure state, (ii) running unitary evolution on the combined system, (iii) ignoring (tracing out) the environment afterwards. The size of E required is bounded — for a d-dimensional system you never need an environment larger than d² — so this isn't a trick that hides infinities.

**What this means physically.** All the irreversibility in quantum mechanics is in step (iii), the partial trace. Steps (i) and (ii) are unitary, hence reversible. The information that the channel "loses" hasn't actually been destroyed — it's been transferred into the environment, and the irreversibility is purely a consequence of our choosing to ignore the environmental degrees of freedom. If you kept track of the environment, the entire process would be a reversible unitary evolution on S⊗E.

A simple example fixes the picture. Consider phase-damping on a qubit: |0⟩⟨0| stays put, |1⟩⟨1| stays put, but the off-diagonal coherences |0⟩⟨1| and |1⟩⟨0| decay to zero. This is the textbook decoherence channel and it's clearly irreversible — pure superpositions become classical mixtures. Stinespring's dilation: introduce an auxiliary qubit in |0⟩_E, apply the controlled-NOT U = |0⟩⟨0|_S ⊗ I_E + |1⟩⟨1|_S ⊗ X_E, then trace out E. The CNOT is unitary; the trace is what destroys the coherence. The off-diagonals didn't vanish — they got copied into correlations between S and E, and they're still there in the joint state. They only *look* gone if you refuse to look at E.

**Why this is exactly the framework's FANOUT picture.** The CNOT in that example is, structurally, a FANOUT operation: it copies the computational-basis information of S into E. After the CNOT, the joint state has S's classical-basis information redundantly recorded in two places. That redundant recording is what makes the partial trace destroy the coherence — you can't have a coherent superposition between |0⟩_S|0⟩_E and |1⟩_S|1⟩_E once you've stopped tracking E, because the two branches are now distinguishable by E's state. Decoherence *is* FANOUT in the framework's vocabulary, and Stinespring's theorem says this is the *only* way irreversibility happens in quantum mechanics. Every CPTP map is a FANOUT-into-environment followed by ignoring the environment.

This is what licenses the framework's claim that pre-FANOUT evolution is unitary as a theorem rather than a stipulation. It isn't an extra postulate — it's the contrapositive of Stinespring. If no FANOUT has occurred (no environmental copy has been made), then no partial trace is being performed, then the evolution is the unitary U on S⊗E with E in its initial state, then it's reversible. The moment FANOUT happens, the partial trace becomes operationally meaningful (because there's now a record in E that defines what "ignoring E" means), and irreversibility appears. The "boundary" the framework keeps identifying — between unitary and non-unitary, reversible and irreversible, pre-measurement and post-measurement — is operationally the boundary where the partial trace acquires meaning, and that's the FANOUT event.

**The reason this isn't well-known outside quantum information theory.** Stinespring's 1955 paper was written in the language of operator algebras and C*-algebras, and for decades it lived in mathematical physics rather than foundations. Kraus reformulated it in 1971 as the operator-sum decomposition E(ρ) = Σ_k K_k ρ K_k† with Σ_k K_k† K_k = I — this is what physicists usually call "Kraus operators" or "the operator-sum representation", and most quantum information textbooks introduce the result in that form. The Stinespring and Kraus formulations are mathematically equivalent (the K_k are just the matrix elements of U between |0⟩_E and a basis of E), but the Stinespring version is the one that makes the dilation structure transparent: *system + environment + unitary + trace*. That's the form the framework wants, because the partial trace is where FANOUT lives.

The mathematical class that captures all such operations is **completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) maps**, sometimes called *quantum channels*. So a quantum channel is a map E: ρ ↦ E(ρ) on density matrices that's (i) linear, (ii) trace-preserving (probabilities still sum to 1), and (iii) completely positive (positive even when applied to part of an entangled system — the "complete" rules out the transpose map, which is positive but unphysical).

**The theorem (Stinespring 1955).** Every CPTP map E acting on a system S can be written as

  E(ρ_S) = Tr_E [ U (ρ_S ⊗ |0⟩⟨0|_E) U† ]

where E is an auxiliary "environment" Hilbert space, |0⟩_E is some fixed pure state, U is a *unitary* on the joint system S⊗E, and Tr_E is the partial trace over E. In words: any quantum operation, however irreversible-looking, can be implemented by (i) attaching a fresh environment in a known pure state, (ii) running unitary evolution on the combined system, (iii) ignoring (tracing out) the environment afterwards. The size of E required is bounded — for a d-dimensional system you never need an environment larger than d² — so this isn't a trick that hides infinities.

**What this means physically.** All the irreversibility in quantum mechanics is in step (iii), the partial trace. Steps (i) and (ii) are unitary, hence reversible. The information that the channel "loses" hasn't actually been destroyed — it's been transferred into the environment, and the irreversibility is purely a consequence of our choosing to ignore the environmental degrees of freedom. If you kept track of the environment, the entire process would be a reversible unitary evolution on S⊗E.

A simple example fixes the picture. Consider phase-damping on a qubit: |0⟩⟨0| stays put, |1⟩⟨1| stays put, but the off-diagonal coherences |0⟩⟨1| and |1⟩⟨0| decay to zero. The CNOT is unitary; the trace is what destroys the coherence. The off-diagonals didn't vanish — they got copied into correlations between S and E, and they're still there in the joint state. They only *look* gone if you refuse to look at E.
